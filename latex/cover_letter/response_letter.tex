\noindent \textbf{Reviewer \#1:} ``If this is intended as a method suitable for
genomic epidemiology applications in future pandemics, it needs to be
evaluated against a much broader range of plausible pandemic-causing pathogens
with the limitations and recommendations clearly established.''

\begin{itemize}
  \item Same as the editor's priority point.
  \item Done: sc2ts has been generalised to other pathogens (reference genome and date
are now configuration parameters), and we have evaluated it in contrasting parameter
regimes with simulations where the truth is known (below).
  \item Done: we simulated epidemics forwards in time with SLiM under a neutral
Wright--Fisher model with recombination, for a coronavirus-like pathogen (30\,kb,
$8\times10^{-4}$ substitutions/site/year, 5.5 day generations, $\sim$3 years) and an
influenza-like pathogen modelled on pdm2009 H1N1 (13\,kb, $2.75\times10^{-3}$
substitutions/site/year, daily generations, growing to $10^6$ cases per day over 100 days),
each at two sampling densities. We ran sc2ts with $k = 3, 4, 5$ and scored the inferred
recombinants, breakpoints and the overall ARG against the true ARG
(Methods, ``Simulation of pathogen epidemics''; SI ``Simulations of pathogen epidemics'';
\texttt{simulations/slim}, \texttt{notebooks/analysis\_slim\_pathogen\_simulations.ipynb}).
  \item Results \todo{provisional numbers; update from final runs}:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Inferred recombinants are almost always genuine: precision 0.94--1 for the
coronavirus-like pathogen and 0.89--1 for the influenza-like pathogen (0.72 at $k=3$ with
the densest sampling).
      \item Detection is limited mainly by the diversity available: with $k = 4$, about
50--65\% of recombination events are detected for the coronavirus-like pathogen, but fewer
than 10\% in the first 100 days of the influenza-like epidemic, where the parents of most
recombinants differ at only a few sites. A SARS-CoV-like pathogen with the same demography
gave essentially no detectable recombinants. This is a limit of the information in the
sequences rather than of sc2ts.
      \item Events seen only through sampled descendants of an unsampled recombinant are
detected at least as often as those whose recombinant was sampled.
      \item The true breakpoint lies in the inferred interval for 90--98\% (coronavirus-like)
and 78--90\% (influenza-like) of detected events.
    \end{itemize}
  \item Limitations and recommendations for other pathogens, from these and the synthetic
recombinant simulations (SI ``Implications for other pathogens''; Discussion):
    \begin{itemize}
      \item When to trust calls: across all regimes, a recombinant inferred by sc2ts is
almost always genuine. Precision drops only with a small $k$, dense sampling and little
diversity.
      \item When to expect misses: recombination between closely related parents, as early
in an epidemic or in a slowly evolving pathogen, is largely undetectable by sc2ts or any
sequence-based method. Detection improves as co-circulating lineages diverge.
      \item Unsampled recombinants and parents: events seen only through sampled
descendants are detected as often as sampled ones; mutations on unsampled parents barely
affect detection.
      \item Choosing $k$: a smaller $k$ recovers more events in low-diversity settings at
some cost in precision; the Methods (``Choice of k parameter'') give a procedure for
choosing $k$ for a new pathogen.
      \item Not covered: reassortment in segmented genomes, and sequencing artefacts
(the simulations are error-free).
    \end{itemize}
  \item Done (first draft): the Discussion now states these limitations, with details in
the SI. \todo{Expand into fuller recommendations once final simulation results are in.}
\end{itemize}
