\opening{Dear Dr Li,}

We thank you and the reviewers for the careful and constructive evaluation of
our Article, ``A Pandemic-Scale Ancestral Recombination Graph for SARS-CoV-2''.
We have substantially revised the manuscript in response, with major updates
both to the SARS-CoV-2 ARG itself and to the evaluation of sc2ts as a method
for future pathogen genomic datasets.

First, we have rebuilt the ARG using all of the latest Viridian data, extending
the resource to 3,114,609 genomes sampled through 6 June 2024, and have updated
all analyses accordingly. We also made particular efforts to improve the
reconstruction of the early histories of Delta and Omicron. Second,
addressing the editor’s priority and related comments from both reviewers
to establish the applicability and
limitations of sc2ts beyond SARS-CoV-2, we have generalised the method so that
it is no longer specific to the SARS-CoV-2 reference genome, and added two
complementary simulation studies. These quantify recombinant detection under
controlled SARS-CoV-2 sequence backgrounds and evaluate end-to-end ARG
inference in forward-time simulations of contrasting coronavirus-like and
influenza-like epidemic regimes, where the true ARG is known.

Together, these analyses allow us to state more clearly where sc2ts performs
well and where its limitations lie. In particular, inferred recombinants are
generally reliable, whereas sensitivity is strongly limited when little genetic
diversity has accumulated between co-circulating lineages; the simulations also
provide an empirical basis for choosing the recombination penalty for new
pathogens. We have correspondingly revised the manuscript to make its intended
users and scope more explicit, to distinguish corroboration from validation
where the truth is unknown, and to address the reviewers' specific concerns
throughout.

We have included a copy of the manuscript in which all changes have been
highlighted.

We respond to each point in turn below.

\noindent \textbf{Reviewer \#1:} \textit{``This manuscript introduces a method for

the ARG, and that the accompanying documentation makes the work reproducible.
At the same time, the reviewer's well-founded critiques identified several important
ways in which the manuscript could be strengthened. In response, we have
substantially revised both the resource and the methodological evaluation: we
have rebuilt the ARG using all available Viridian data, clarified the intended
users and scope of the resource, and added extensive simulations in which the
true ancestry is known. These changes allow us to quantify more directly both
the strengths and limitations of sc2ts across contrasting epidemic regimes. We
have also adopted more circumspect language throughout and addressed the
reviewer’s specific concerns about co-circulation, recurrent multi-nucleotide

\noindent \textbf{Reviewer \#1:} \textit{``Most critically, I wonder who this is for
exactly? As a resource, I have substantial misgivings about whether applied

Phylogenetic trees are already challenging in many public health

We agree with the reviewer that the primary users of this resource are likely
to be researchers---including evolutionary biologists, epidemiologists, lineage
curators and methods developers---rather than routine applied public-health
surveillance. We have revised the Discussion to make this intended user base
explicit and to give concrete examples of how the ARG could be used. We have
also clarified our use of ``real time''. This refers to the inference method,

\noindent \textbf{Reviewer \#1:} \textit{``If this is intended as a method suitable for
genomic epidemiology applications in future pandemics, it needs to be
evaluated against a much broader range of plausible pandemic-causing pathogens
with the limitations and recommendations clearly established.''}

We agree, and this comment motivated one of the major additions to the revised
manuscript. We have first generalised sc2ts so that the reference genome and
reference date are configurable and the method is no longer specific to
SARS-CoV-2. We have then added two complementary simulation studies. Synthetic
recombinants constructed from real SARS-CoV-2 genomes allow us to quantify how
performance depends on parental divergence, the recombination penalty $k$, and
divergence from the closest sampled parents. In addition, we ran sc2ts on
forward-time simulations of coronavirus-like and influenza-like epidemics
spanning contrasting genome lengths, mutation rates, generation times, epidemic
sizes and sampling densities, with the true ARG known. Together, these
simulations establish the principal strengths and limitations of the method.
Recombinants inferred by sc2ts are generally reliable, while sensitivity is
strongly reduced when little genetic diversity has accumulated between parental
lineages, particularly early in an epidemic. The simulations also quantify the
precision--recall trade-off controlled by $k$ and provide a practical procedure
for selecting $k$ for a new pathogen. We now state these limitations and
recommendations explicitly in the manuscript.

\noindent \textbf{Major}

\noindent \textbf{Reviewer \#1:} \textit{``The post hoc examination of factors
consistent with increased recombination rates (case rates and viral genetic
diversity) seems reasonable, but should be much more precise. Specifically,
recombination requires that viral lineages co-circulate in space and time.
Given that most samples will have both a sampling date and a location, this
analysis should be much more granular and could be substantially more
convincing. Do the identified precise parental lineages co-circulate in region
and time? Additionally, given that recombination between closely related
samples will likely not be detectable by this and other methods, the
relationship between lineage diversity and recombination rates might be
expected as a consequence of the inference procedure. While I still think this
is useful to consider, the explanation and discussion could be more
nuanced.''}

We agree with both aspects of this comment and have added analyses to address
them directly.

First, we now test whether the inferred parents of recombinant events are
closer in time and space than expected by chance. For the 224 QC-passing
recombinants for which both inferred parents are sampled genomes, the parents
were sampled a median of 25 days apart and 60\% were sampled in the same
country. Because the dataset is strongly geographically structured, we compared
these values with a null in which parents were exchanged between recombinants
of similar date, preserving the sampling composition through time. Under this
null, parents were sampled a median of 41 days apart and 40\% were from the same
country; both differences were significant in 1,000 permutations ($p < 0.001$).
The inferred parents are therefore closer in time and space than expected by
chance, consistent with co-circulation of the parental lineages. We have added
this analysis to the Results, Methods and Supplementary Information.

Second, we agree that the observed association between lineage diversity and
inferred recombination is expected in part from the inference procedure itself.
The new simulations now quantify this directly: with $k = 4$, 89\% of synthetic
Alpha$\times$Delta recombinants were detected, compared with only 40\% of
Alpha$\times$Alpha recombinants whose parents differ at relatively few sites.
The forward-time simulations show the same effect across whole epidemics. We
have therefore revised the relevant Results section to state explicitly that
the relationship between diversity and inferred recombination reflects both
biological opportunity for recombination and variation in our ability to detect
it, and that these effects cannot be cleanly separated in the empirical data.

\noindent \textbf{Reviewer \#1:} \textit{``There has been some research in the effect
of recurrent mutli-nucleotide mutations with convincing examples at quite a
few sites (see de Maio et al.
\url{https://academic.oup.com/mbe/article/42/11/msaf272/8301027}). I would
expect that sc2ts could be impacted by these events resulting in elevated
rates of inferred recombination. This should be addressed and if possible
mitigated.''}

We thank the reviewer for drawing our attention to this issue. We have now
examined explicitly whether recurrent multi-nucleotide substitutions (MNS) can
generate spurious recombination signals.

Our existing QC measure already reduces this problem by grouping closely spaced
supporting sites into a single locus, so that clustered substitutions do not
contribute multiple independent units of support. We additionally checked all
single-breakpoint recombination events against the 19 highly recurrent MNS
catalogued by De Maio et al. Recurrent MNS can contribute apparent support for
recombination, but the QC procedure largely prevents them from being sufficient
to support a recombinant call. Among the 647 QC-passing recombination events,
discounting recurrent MNS would cause only seven additional events to fall
below the QC threshold, whereas all 42 events involving the particularly
recurrent MNS at positions 27875--27883 fail QC. We have added these results to
the manuscript and now cite De Maio et al. in the discussion of this QC
measure.

\noindent \textbf{Reviewer \#1:} \textit{``The SARS-CoV-2 pandemic has largely come and
gone and global sequencing efforts are in free-fall (note that this resource
is not updated with the most recently produced data). While I appreciate the
tool could be `real time' in a new pandemic, there's no telling exactly what
the next pandemic will resemble. If this is going to be a meaningful method
going forward, I suggest this should be evaluated across simulations
encompassing varied plausible parameters. E.g., a flu pandemic is thought to
be likely, could this be used to handle the kinds of reassortment that we see
with segmented genomes?''}

As described in response to the reviewer’s earlier comment, this concern
motivated the new simulation studies. In particular, the forward-time
simulations include an influenza-like epidemic regime modelled on pandemic H1N1
parameters as well as a coronavirus-like regime, with two sampling densities
and several values of $k$. Because the true ARG is known, these simulations allow
us to evaluate recombinant detection, breakpoint inference and the ARG as a
whole under conditions very different from those of SARS-CoV-2. The
influenza-like simulations also make an important limitation clear: during the
early stages of a rapidly growing epidemic, relatively little sequence
diversity may have accumulated even when the annual substitution rate is high.
Under these conditions, recombinant parents often differ at too few sites for
their ancestry to be distinguished confidently, and recall is correspondingly
low.
The current implementation of sc2ts does not, however, model reassortment
in segmented genomes. The HMM currently treats the genome as a single linear
sequence and applies the same recombination penalty to a change of parent at
any position. Reassortment would require an extension in which switches at
segment boundaries are assigned little or no penalty while switches within a
segment remain rare. The ARG representation itself can accommodate such
ancestry, but the inference model would need to be modified. We now state this
explicitly as a limitation and direction for future work.


\noindent \textbf{Reviewer \#1:} \textit{``In parts of the introduction and results,
the overall tone is one that suggests this method has solved genealogical
inference for SARS-CoV-2 and to a casual reader might indicate very accurate
genealogical inference. While I do think some aspects are improved by this

removed claims of accuracy where these were supported only by comparisons
between alternative inferences.

The new simulation studies also help address the underlying concern directly,

the conditions under which sensitivity is limited.

and a method with relevance to future pandemics. We also appreciated their
comments on the clarity of the manuscript. Their suggestions on
simulation-based evaluation and on making the choice of inference parameters
more transparent were particularly helpful. Together with the related comments
from Reviewer 1, these helped shape the new simulation analyses in the revised

known and clarify the conditions under which inference becomes difficult. We

\noindent \textbf{Reviewer \#2 (1):} \textit{``Future work. The only major comments I
have deal with future work that I hope will come out of this group.''}

\noindent \textbf{Reviewer \#2 (1a):} \textit{``First, with any novel statistical
method, it is reassuring to assess its behavior on simulated datasets. In this
case, the authors are able to assess the reliability and accuracy of their
method reasonably well by simply relying on the extraordinary wealth of data
on SARS-CoV-2 evolution in lieu of simulations where the truth is known.
However, I think a future project in which the performance of sc2ts is
assessed using viral simulations would be useful, both for quantifying the
method's behavior and identifying areas of parameter space (e.g., rates of
recombination vs.\ mutation, or time-density of sampling, etc.) in which its
performance might become unreliable.''}

We agree that simulations in which the true ancestry is known provide an
important complement to evaluation against empirical SARS-CoV-2 data. We have
therefore added two simulation studies to the revised manuscript.

First, we generated synthetic recombinants from real SARS-CoV-2 genomes. This
allows us to quantify detection as a function of parental divergence and the
recombination penalty k, while also testing the effects of divergence between
the true parents and their closest sampled relatives.

Second, we ran sc2ts on forward-time simulations of complete epidemics under
contrasting coronavirus-like and influenza-like parameter regimes, which differ
in genome length, mutation rate, generation time and epidemic size; for each
regime we also considered two sampling densities and several values of $k$.
Because the full ARG is known in these simulations, we can evaluate recombinant
detection, breakpoint inference and overall ARG reconstruction directly against
the truth. After restricting the comparison to ancestral relationships that can
be resolved from the sequences, 88--94\% of true ancestral nodes are recovered,
and 89--96\% of inferred ancestral nodes correspond to true ancestors.

Together, these studies identify low accumulated diversity between
co-circulating lineages as the principal limitation on recombination detection.
In the forward-time simulations, recombinant calls at $k$ = 4 have high
precision, but many genuine events are missed when parental lineages differ at
only a few sites. We now describe this limitation, and its implications for
applying sc2ts to other pathogens, explicitly in the Results, Discussion and
Supplementary Information.

\noindent \textbf{Reviewer \#2 (1b):} \textit{``Second (although following from 1a
above), it seems like much of the inference done by sc2ts relies on
heuristics. This is understandable, and indeed difficult to avoid in the
analysis of real-world empirical biological data, which is often messy or
unreliable. However, the authors note that a goal of the current work is to
prepare for the next pandemic, so I encourage them to consider publishing a
companion manuscript that walks through the decision-making process involved
in the selection of, e.g., the `k' parameter so that researchers employing
this method in the study of other pathogens might have a playbook off of which
to work.''}

We agree that users applying sc2ts to other pathogens need practical guidance
for choosing parameters rather than simply inheriting the values used for
SARS-CoV-2. The new simulations now provide an empirical basis for this.

For the recombination penalty $k$, the synthetic recombinant simulations quantify
the expected trade-off: increasing $k$ makes recombinant calls more conservative
but reduces sensitivity, particularly when the parents are closely related. For
example, among Alpha$\times$Alpha recombinants, detection falls from 62\% at
$k$ = 3 to 40\% at $k$ = 4 and 21\% at $k$ = 5, while the proportion of detected events
passing our QC criterion rises from 71\% to more than 99\%. The forward-time
simulations show the same general precision--recall trade-off across complete
epidemics.

We have therefore expanded the Methods section on the choice of $k$ into a
practical procedure for applying sc2ts to a new pathogen: construct synthetic
recombinants from representative sequences spanning the parental divergences of
interest, and choose the smallest value of $k$ for which inferred recombinants
are consistently supported by multiple loci on both sides of the breakpoint.

We have also expanded the rationale for other heuristic choices. In particular,
the Methods now explain the HMM-cost threshold used to identify implausibly
divergent samples and the seven-day window used when considering retrospective
insertion of saltational lineages. Our aim is to make the decision-making
underlying these parameters explicit enough that users can adapt them to the
sampling density and evolutionary rate of a different pathogen.

\noindent \textbf{Reviewer \#2 (2):} \textit{``Main Figure: ARGs are often difficult to
parse visually, but I feel this paper would be more compelling with a
graphical demonstration of the inferred relatedness structure enabled by
sc2ts. I wonder if the authors could adapt Figure 2 to display inferred ARG of
SARS-CoV-2? Perhaps the complexity of such a figure could be reduced by

may not be possible (and it's by no means a dealbreaker for me as a reviewer),
but I think it would help demonstrate the power of this approach to a general

We thank the reviewer for this suggestion and agree that graphical
representations of the inferred reticulations are important for conveying what
an ARG adds beyond a conventional phylogeny.

We considered whether the full SARS-CoV-2 ARG could be represented in a single
figure using collapsed clades. However, the revised ARG contains 3.41 million
nodes, of which 1,323 are recombination nodes. Even with extensive collapsing,
a whole-ARG representation would therefore be dominated by the clonal backbone,
while the individual reticulations that distinguish the ARG would be too small
to interpret.

We have instead focused the figures on informative local subgraphs, where the
recombinant structure can be shown directly. Figure 2 displays the inferred ARG
around the XA recombination event and around XQ together with the six Pango X
lineages that descend from it, with clades summarised by sample counts and
genome bars showing the inferred breakpoint intervals. Additional subgraphs
show the origins of Alpha, Delta and Omicron, while Supplementary Data 1 provides
detailed visualisations for recombinant Pango lineages. We think these local
views make the reticulate relationships substantially more interpretable than a
whole-ARG overview would.

We have added a Methods section ``ARG visualisations'' to the revised
manuscript to describe our use of the ``tskit\_arg\_visualizer'' tool
to generate the ARG diagrams presented.
