\begin{letter}{Nature Genetics}

\opening{Dear Dr Li,}

We thank you and the reviewers for the careful and constructive evaluation of
our Article, ``A Pandemic-Scale Ancestral Recombination Graph for SARS-CoV-2''.
We have substantially revised the manuscript in response, with major updates
both to the SARS-CoV-2 ARG itself and to the evaluation of sc2ts as a method
for future pathogen genomic datasets.

First, we have rebuilt the ARG using all of the latest Viridian data, extending
the resource to 3,114,609 genomes sampled through 6 June 2024, and have updated
all analyses accordingly. We also made particular efforts to improve the
reconstruction of the early histories of Delta and Omicron. Second, addressing
the editors' and Reviewer \#1's request to establish the applicability and
limitations of sc2ts beyond SARS-CoV-2, we have generalised the method so that
it is no longer specific to the SARS-CoV-2 reference genome, and added two
complementary simulation studies. These quantify recombinant detection under
controlled SARS-CoV-2 sequence backgrounds and evaluate end-to-end ARG
inference in forward-time simulations of contrasting coronavirus-like and
influenza-like epidemic regimes, where the true ARG is known.

Together, these analyses allow us to state more clearly where sc2ts performs
well and where its limitations lie. In particular, inferred recombinants are
generally reliable, whereas sensitivity is strongly limited when little genetic
diversity has accumulated between co-circulating lineages; the simulations also
provide an empirical basis for choosing the recombination penalty for new
pathogens. We have correspondingly revised the manuscript to make its intended
users and scope more explicit, to distinguish corroboration from validation
where the truth is unknown, and to address the reviewers' specific concerns
throughout.

We have included a copy of the manuscript in which all changes have been
highlighted.

We respond to each point in turn below.

\noindent \textbf{Response to Reviewer \#1}

\noindent \textbf{Reviewer \#1:} \textit{``This manuscript introduces a method for
constructing an ARG from millions of viral genome sequences sampled during the
pandemic. They show that a `real-time' ARG can be constructed at scale and is
similarly or more accurate than existing methods for incorporating
recombination into genealogical inference. Overall, the method is well
described and the analyses are clearly documented and largely appropriate for
the purpose of evaluating the results. I have a few specific analyses that I
suggest below and consider to be essential for investigating the quality of
the resulting ARG and method.''}

We thank the reviewer for their careful and constructive assessment. We
particularly appreciate their positive view that the method is well described,
that the analyses are clearly documented and largely appropriate for evaluating
the ARG, and that the accompanying documentation makes the work reproducible.
At the same time, the reviewer's well-founded critiques identified several important
ways in which the manuscript could be strengthened. In response, we have
substantially revised both the resource and the methodological evaluation: we
have rebuilt the ARG using all available Viridian data, clarified the intended
users and scope of the resource, and added extensive simulations in which the
true ancestry is known. These changes allow us to quantify more directly both
the strengths and limitations of sc2ts across contrasting epidemic regimes. We
have also adopted more circumspect language throughout and addressed the
reviewer’s specific concerns about co-circulation, recurrent multi-nucleotide
substitutions, reassortment, accessibility, and comparison with existing
methods. We believe these changes have significantly improved the manuscript.

\noindent \textbf{Reviewer \#1:} \textit{``Most critically, I wonder who this is for
exactly? As a resource, I have substantial misgivings about whether applied
public health would have any interest or practical ability to use it.
Phylogenetic trees are already challenging in many public health
applications. Furthermore, the ARG provided is not real time because there is
no included plan to update as new data are deposited. It's already far behind
the evolution of SARS-CoV-2 so won't be used soon in this regard.''}

We agree with the reviewer that the primary users of this resource are likely
to be researchers---including evolutionary biologists, epidemiologists, lineage
curators and methods developers---rather than routine applied public-health
surveillance. We have revised the Discussion to make this intended user base
explicit and to give concrete examples of how the ARG could be used. We have
also clarified our use of ``real time''. This refers to the inference method,
which incrementally incorporates samples as they are collected, rather than to
a commitment to maintain the SARS-CoV-2 resource indefinitely. We now frame the
ARG as a stable record of the pandemic phase of SARS-CoV-2. Nevertheless, we
agreed that the resource should be made as current and complete as possible,
and have therefore rebuilt the ARG using all available Viridian data, extending
it to 3,114,609 genomes sampled through 6 June 2024. All analyses in the
manuscript have been updated to this new ARG.

\noindent \textbf{Reviewer \#1:} \textit{``If this is intended as a method suitable for
genomic epidemiology applications in future pandemics, it needs to be
evaluated against a much broader range of plausible pandemic-causing pathogens
with the limitations and recommendations clearly established.''}

We agree, and this comment motivated one of the major additions to the revised
manuscript. We have first generalised sc2ts so that the reference genome and
reference date are configurable and the method is no longer specific to
SARS-CoV-2. We have then added two complementary simulation studies. Synthetic
recombinants constructed from real SARS-CoV-2 genomes allow us to quantify how
performance depends on parental divergence, the recombination penalty $k$, and
divergence from the closest sampled parents. In addition, we ran sc2ts on
forward-time simulations of coronavirus-like and influenza-like epidemics
spanning contrasting genome lengths, mutation rates, generation times, epidemic
sizes and sampling densities, with the true ARG known. Together, these
simulations establish the principal strengths and limitations of the method.
Recombinants inferred by sc2ts are generally reliable, while sensitivity is
strongly reduced when little genetic diversity has accumulated between parental
lineages, particularly early in an epidemic. The simulations also quantify the
precision--recall trade-off controlled by $k$ and provide a practical procedure
for selecting $k$ for a new pathogen. We now state these limitations and
recommendations explicitly in the manuscript.

\noindent \large{\textbf{Major}}

\noindent \textbf{Reviewer \#1:} \textit{``The post hoc examination of factors
consistent with increased recombination rates (case rates and viral genetic
diversity) seems reasonable, but should be much more precise. Specifically,
recombination requires that viral lineages co-circulate in space and time.
Given that most samples will have both a sampling date and a location, this
analysis should be much more granular and could be substantially more
convincing. Do the identified precise parental lineages co-circulate in region
and time? Additionally, given that recombination between closely related
samples will likely not be detectable by this and other methods, the
relationship between lineage diversity and recombination rates might be
expected as a consequence of the inference procedure. While I still think this
is useful to consider, the explanation and discussion could be more
nuanced.''}

We agree with both aspects of this comment and have added analyses to address
them directly.

First, we now test whether the inferred parents of recombinant events
co-circulate in time and space. For the 224 QC-passing recombinants for which
both inferred parents are sampled genomes, the parents were sampled a median of
25 days apart and 60\% were sampled in the same country. Because the dataset is
strongly geographically structured, we compared these values with a null in
which parents were exchanged between recombinants of similar date, preserving
the sampling composition through time. Under this null, parents were sampled a
median of 41 days apart and 40\% were from the same country; both differences
were significant in 1,000 permutations ($p < 0.001$). We have added this analysis
to the Results, Methods and Supplementary Information.

Second, we agree that the observed association between lineage diversity and
inferred recombination is expected in part from the inference procedure itself.
The new simulations now quantify this directly: with $k = 4$, 89\% of synthetic
Alpha$\times$Delta recombinants were detected, compared with only 40\% of
Alpha$\times$Alpha recombinants whose parents differ at relatively few sites.
The forward-time simulations show the same effect across whole epidemics. We
have therefore revised the relevant Results section to state explicitly that
the relationship between diversity and inferred recombination reflects both
biological opportunity for recombination and variation in our ability to detect
it, and that these effects cannot be cleanly separated in the empirical data.

\noindent \textbf{Reviewer \#1:} \textit{``There has been some research in the effect
of recurrent mutli-nucleotide mutations with convincing examples at quite a
few sites (see de Maio et al.
\url{https://academic.oup.com/mbe/article/42/11/msaf272/8301027}). I would
expect that sc2ts could be impacted by these events resulting in elevated
rates of inferred recombination. This should be addressed and if possible
mitigated.''}

We thank the reviewer for drawing our attention to this issue. We have now
examined explicitly whether recurrent multi-nucleotide substitutions (MNS) can
generate spurious recombination signals.

Our existing QC measure already reduces this problem by grouping closely spaced
supporting sites into a single locus, so that clustered substitutions do not
contribute multiple independent units of support. We additionally checked all
single-breakpoint recombination events against the 19 highly recurrent MNS
catalogued by De Maio et al. Recurrent MNS can indeed produce apparent
recombinant matches, but these events are largely removed by the existing QC
procedure. Among the 647 QC-passing recombination events, discounting recurrent
MNS would cause only seven additional events to fall below the QC threshold,
whereas all 42 events involving the particularly recurrent MNS at positions
27875--27883 fail QC. We have added these results to the manuscript and now
cite De Maio et al. in the discussion of this QC measure.


\noindent \textbf{Reviewer \#1:} \textit{``The SARS-CoV-2 pandemic has largely come and
gone and global sequencing efforts are in free-fall (note that this resource
is not updated with the most recently produced data). While I appreciate the
tool could be `real time' in a new pandemic, there's no telling exactly what
the next pandemic will resemble. If this is going to be a meaningful method
going forward, I suggest this should be evaluated across simulations
encompassing varied plausible parameters. E.g., a flu pandemic is thought to
be likely, could this be used to handle the kinds of reassortment that we see
with segmented genomes?''}

As described in response to the reviewer’s earlier comment, this concern
motivated the new simulation studies. In particular, the forward-time
simulations include an influenza-like epidemic regime modelled on pandemic H1N1
parameters as well as a coronavirus-like regime, with two sampling densities
and several values of $k$. Because the true ARG is known, these simulations allow
us to evaluate recombinant detection, breakpoint inference and the ARG as a
whole under conditions very different from those of SARS-CoV-2. The
influenza-like simulations also make an important limitation clear: during the
early stages of a rapidly growing epidemic, relatively little sequence
diversity may have accumulated even when the annual substitution rate is high.
Under these conditions, recombinant parents often differ at too few sites for
their ancestry to be distinguished confidently, and recall is correspondingly
low. The current implementation of sc2ts does not, however, model reassortment
in segmented genomes. The HMM currently treats the genome as a single linear
sequence and applies the same recombination penalty to a change of parent at
any position. Reassortment would require an extension in which switches at
segment boundaries are assigned little or no penalty while switches within a
segment remain rare. The ARG representation itself can accommodate such
ancestry, but the inference model would need to be modified. We now state this
explicitly as a limitation and direction for future work.


\noindent \textbf{Reviewer \#1:} \textit{``In parts of the introduction and results,
the overall tone is one that suggests this method has solved genealogical
inference for SARS-CoV-2 and to a casual reader might indicate very accurate
genealogical inference. While I do think some aspects are improved by this
work, the uncertainty is still enormous, all `validations' applied to real
data are `corroborative analyses' in reality. This work would benefit from a
substantive effort to soften and inject more circumspect phrasing
throughout.''}

We agree with this criticism and have adopted more circumspect language
throughout the manuscript.

In particular, comparisons against real data, other inference methods and
results from the literature are now described as corroboration or as being
consistent with previous results, rather than as validation. We reserve
``validation'' for settings in which the truth is known,
principally the new simulations. We have similarly
removed claims of accuracy where these were supported only by comparisons
between alternative inferences.

The new simulation studies also help address the underlying concern directly,
because they allow us to quantify both where sc2ts performs well and where
inference becomes unreliable when compared with known truth.


\noindent \textbf{Reviewer \#1:} \textit{``Some recommendations should be provided for
the expected practical use cases. Much of the text suggests this will be used
by researchers -- certainly I would expect population geneticists and the like
to be interested. I don't think this has much practical utility in applied
public health where it is already a struggle to incorporate single phylogenies
and instead we use broad-scale categories for analysis and reporting concerns.
If this is a new method and resource, it is critical that we establish who the
intended user-base is in reality and how they would interact with it to
address their concerns.''}

As noted above, we agree that the main users of the ARG are likely to be
researchers rather than routine public-health surveillance teams, and we now
state this explicitly.

We have also made clearer how researchers can interact with the resource. The
ARG can be loaded and queried through the tskit Python API on modest hardware,
and tabular summaries of nodes, samples and Pango assignments can be generated
efficiently for downstream analyses. In the Discussion we give examples of
potential applications, including combining the ARG with experimentally derived
fitness estimates, identifying saltational lineages, and developing
recombination-aware methods for pathogen genomic analysis.

\noindent \textbf{Response to Reviewer \#2}

\noindent \textbf{Reviewer \#2:} ``Summary: In this manuscript, the authors
present both an extraordinary data resource -- an inferred Ancestral
Recombination Graph (ARG) for SARS-CoV-2 -- as well as a statistical method --
sc2ts -- for inferring ARGs using time-series sampling of pandemic-scale
genomic data. This method builds off a widely used model in population
genetics, the Li and Stephens Hidden Markov Model, that is tweaked here to
rely only on a `recombination penalty' parameter, the value of which is
determined heuristically. The inferred ARG is rigorously compared and
sanity-checked against what is known (or has been previously inferred) about
the history of the genomic evolution of SARS-CoV-2.''

\noindent ``Opinion: Although recombination between SARS-CoV-2 lineages is
rare, it has unambiguously occurred, which means that the true genetic history
of the virus is best understood as a reticulate genealogy, i.e., an ARG. An
accurate genetic history, as well as tools for inferring such histories, are
crucial not only to fundamental questions in epidemiology and evolution, but
also to the development of appropriate measures for mitigating the spread of
human pathogens. The work presented in this manuscript represents a clear step
forward as both an accessible data resource for our understanding of the
evolution of the modern diversity of SARS-CoV-2 and as a method for
understanding the evolutionary trajectory of future pandemics. The manuscript
is clear and well-written, and I had only minor suggestions for increasing the
clarity and impact of the work.''

\begin{itemize}
  \item Thank the reviewer for the very positive assessment.
\end{itemize}

\noindent \textbf{Reviewer \#2 (1):} ``Future work. The only major comments I
have deal with future work that I hope will come out of this group.''

\noindent \textbf{Reviewer \#2 (1a):} ``First, with any novel statistical
method, it is reassuring to assess its behavior on simulated datasets. In this
case, the authors are able to assess the reliability and accuracy of their
method reasonably well by simply relying on the extraordinary wealth of data
on SARS-CoV-2 evolution in lieu of simulations where the truth is known.
However, I think a future project in which the performance of sc2ts is
assessed using viral simulations would be useful, both for quantifying the
method's behavior and identifying areas of parameter space (e.g., rates of
recombination vs.\ mutation, or time-density of sampling, etc.) in which its
performance might become unreliable.''

\begin{itemize}
  \item Done: we have added a simulation study of synthetic recombinants spliced from
real sequences, which quantifies false positive and detection rates as a function of
the recombination penalty $k$, the divergence between the parents, and the number of
mutations separating the parents from their closest sampled relatives (Methods,
``Simulation of synthetic recombinants''; SI ``Synthetic recombinant simulations'').
  \item Done: we have also added a forward-time simulation study (SLiM) of epidemics of a
coronavirus-like and an influenza-like pathogen, in which the true ARG is known. These vary
the mutation rate, recombination rate, population size, generation time and sampling density
(samples per day), as the reviewer suggests. Together, the two studies identify the areas
of parameter space where performance becomes unreliable. sc2ts's inferred recombinants are
almost always genuine throughout; false positives appear only with a small $k$, dense
sampling and little diversity. False negatives dominate when there is little accumulated
diversity between parents, such as in the early stages of an epidemic
(Methods, ``Simulation of pathogen epidemics''; SI ``Simulations of pathogen epidemics'').
\todo{Update with final numbers; consider varying the recombination rate and simulation
length more systematically.}
\end{itemize}

\noindent \textbf{Reviewer \#2 (1b):} ``Second (although following from 1a
above), it seems like much of the inference done by sc2ts relies on
heuristics. This is understandable, and indeed difficult to avoid in the
analysis of real-world empirical biological data, which is often messy or
unreliable. However, the authors note that a goal of the current work is to
prepare for the next pandemic, so I encourage them to consider publishing a
companion manuscript that walks through the decision-making process involved
in the selection of, e.g., the `k' parameter so that researchers employing
this method in the study of other pathogens might have a playbook off of which
to work.''

\begin{itemize}
  \item The simulations now quantify the trade-off governed by $k$: as $k$
increases from 3 to 5, detection falls (Alpha$\times$Alpha: 62\% $\to$ 21\%)
while the fraction of detected recombinants passing QC rises (71\% $\to$
100\%), providing an empirical basis for $k = 4$.
  \item The Methods section ``Choice of k parameter'' now describes this, and sets out
the procedure as a playbook for other pathogens: simulate recombinants from real
sequences spanning the parental divergences of interest, and choose the smallest $k$
at which detected events are consistently supported on both sides of the breakpoint.
  \item The SLiM simulations show the same trade-off on whole simulated epidemics: as $k$
increases, recall falls and precision rises. They also show that the best $k$ depends on
the diversity available: with little diversity, $k = 3$ recovers several times as many
events as $k = 4$, at some cost in precision. \todo{Add to the playbook in ``Choice of k
parameter'' once final numbers are in.}
  \item Done: the Methods (``Filtering time travellers'') now explain the choice of the HMM
cost threshold. With dense daily sampling, a sample should differ from a close relative
already in the ARG by only a few mutations, and a cost of 7 corresponds to several months
of unsampled evolution. Because samples above the threshold can still be added
retrospectively, we chose a low threshold. The synthetic recombinant simulations quantify
the cost: recombinants whose parents were not sampled are more often held back.
  \item Done: the Methods (``Inserting saltational lineages'') now also explain the
seven-day window for retrospective inclusion. It is long enough for a genuinely emerging
lineage to accumulate support under dense sampling, while keeping the delay before it is
added short, and making it unlikely that sporadic time travellers form a coherent group.
\end{itemize}

\noindent \textbf{Reviewer \#2 (2):} ``Main Figure: ARGs are often difficult to
parse visually, but I feel this paper would be more compelling with a
graphical demonstration of the inferred relatedness structure enabled by
sc2ts. I wonder if the authors could adapt Figure 2 to display inferred ARG of
SARS-CoV-2? Perhaps the complexity of such a figure could be reduced by
collapsing some clades into triangles, as is often done in the phylogenetics
literature, in order to emphasize the important inferred reticulations? This
may not be possible (and it's by no means a dealbreaker for me as a reviewer),
but I think it would help demonstrate the power of this approach to a general
audience.''

\begin{itemize}
  \item We thank the reviewer for this suggestion, and agree that showing the inferred
reticulations graphically is the best way to convey what an ARG adds.
  \item We considered a whole-ARG figure, but do not think it would serve this purpose.
The ARG has 3.41 million nodes, of which 1,323 are recombination nodes. Even with clades
collapsed into triangles, a figure at this scale would be dominated by the clonal backbone.
That backbone is already familiar from existing SARS-CoV-2 phylogenies, and is congruent
with the UShER tree (Results, tanglegrams). The individual reticulations, most of which
involve small clades, would not be legible.
  \item We have therefore focused on subgraphs, where the reticulations can be shown in
detail:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Figure~2 shows the subgraphs around the recombination nodes for XA, and for XQ
together with the six Pango X lineages descending from it. Clades are summarised by their
sample counts, and the genome bars show the breakpoint intervals.
      \item The Supplementary Figures show the subgraphs at the origins of Alpha, Delta and
Omicron.
      \item Document S1 gives detailed visualisations of the recombination events
underlying the Pango X lineages.
    \end{itemize}
  \item Readers can also extract subgraphs of interest and explore them interactively with
tskit visualisation tools such as tskit\_arg\_visualizer, which we have used extensively
for this purpose (see also our response to Reviewer \#1 on Lorax).
\end{itemize}
